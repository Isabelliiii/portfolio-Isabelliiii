Overview

Philadelphia's housing market reflects deep spatial inequality — value is concentrated in certain districts while lower-income neighbourhoods remain underserved. This project models residential property sale prices to understand the structural, demographic, and spatial drivers of housing value and to examine whether the city's assessment system distributes tax burdens equitably.

Data covers 2023–2024 single-family sales from the Office of Property Assessment, joined with ACS census demographics, crime records, transit stops, recreation facilities, and hospital locations.

Methodology

01

Structural Features Only

Livable area, bedrooms, bathrooms, house age (+ quadratic term). R² = 0.45. Larger homes and more bathrooms raise price; older homes depreciate but the decline slows over time.

02

+ Census Variables

Adds median income, BA rate, unemployment, Black population share. R² rises to 0.60. Neighbourhood socioeconomic context explains a large share of price variation beyond physical attributes.

03

+ Spatial Features

Adds crime count, transit stops, recreation count, hospital proximity. R² = 0.61. Transit access and recreation show strong positive effects; hospital proximity matters with diminishing returns.

04

+ Interactions & Fixed Effects

Adds garage dummy, Center City indicator, recreation level, crime × unemployment interaction. R² = 0.61. Crime impact on price varies by economic context: stronger in low-unemployment areas.

Key Findings

Strongest Predictor

Total livable area has the largest positive standardised coefficient — larger homes consistently sell for more, even after controlling for neighbourhood and location.

Neighbourhood Context

Higher income and education levels raise prices. Higher unemployment and larger Black population shares correspond to lower values, reflecting structural inequalities.

Accessibility Matters

Transit access and recreation facilities show strong positive effects. Proximity to hospitals adds value. Crime has limited direct effect once socioeconomic factors are controlled.

Spatial Equity Issue

Residual maps show high-value wards are consistently underestimated (actual > predicted), suggesting potential under-assessment. Lower-income wards tend to be overestimated — consistent with regressive assessment.

Conclusions & Recommendations

Philadelphia's housing values are spatially organised along socioeconomic lines. The model's residual pattern points to a potential systemic bias in property assessment: wealthier areas appear undertaxed relative to true market values, while lower-income areas may face over-assessment.

In high-unemployment neighbourhoods, housing markets no longer respond to safety improvements in isolation — policies must address the underlying economic base through job creation, small business support, and infrastructure investment. Future work should integrate temporal dynamics and machine learning to improve accuracy at both ends of the price distribution.
